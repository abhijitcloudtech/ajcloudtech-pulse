## Chemistry's Latest Milestone: Unraveling Molecular Handedness with the 2026 Nobel Prize

October 17, 2026 – The world of chemistry is abuzz with the recent announcement of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to French chemist Henri B. Kagan and Japanese chemist Kenso Soai. Their groundbreaking work on "non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis" has provided a long-sought solution to the mystery of molecular chirality, often referred to as "molecular handedness."

Many molecules, like our own hands, exist in two forms that are mirror images of each other but cannot be superimposed. These are called chiral molecules. Intriguingly, living organisms almost exclusively utilize only one of these mirror-image variants—a phenomenon known as homochirality. For instance, in proteins, only "left-handed" amino acids are found.

For over a century, chemists faced a significant challenge: when synthesizing chiral molecules in the lab, they typically produced equal proportions of both mirror images. However, in applications such as pharmaceuticals, often only one specific mirror image has the desired therapeutic effect, while the other can be inactive or even harmful (a historical example being Thalidomide). The ability to selectively produce one "handed" form has been a crucial quest.

Henri B. Kagan took the decisive first step in 1986, discovering a new way to manipulate chemical reactions to create a greater excess of one mirror image than previously thought possible. Kenso Soai further advanced this field in 1995 and 2003, designing the first chemical reaction capable of being homochiral, ultimately achieving a reaction where only one of the two possible mirror images was formed.

Their discoveries have fundamentally reshaped our understanding of how homochirality can emerge spontaneously and have been pivotal for chemists in designing reactions for the manufacture of pharmaceuticals and other essential materials. This monumental achievement offers not only practical benefits in drug development but also profound insights into the very origins of life's chemistry.
