## Chemistry's Green Revolution: Enzymes Tackle Plastic Waste with Unprecedented Efficiency

**November 14, 2026** – The fight against plastic pollution is witnessing a pivotal moment in chemistry, with recent breakthroughs in enzymatic recycling dramatically reshaping the future of waste management. Gone are the days when chemical recycling was deemed too costly or energy-intensive; innovative processes are now making enzyme-based depolymerization not just environmentally sustainable, but also economically competitive with producing virgin plastics.

A landmark study from June 2025, involving an international team including scientists from the U.S. Department of Energy's National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL), the University of Massachusetts Lowell, and the University of Portsmouth, unveiled a revolutionary approach to recycling polyethylene terephthalate (PET). This new process slashed annual running costs by an astounding 74% and reduced energy use by 65%. Crucially, it cut expensive acid and base additions by over 99%, achieving this by replacing sodium hydroxide with recyclable ammonium hydroxide. This subtle chemical switch created a closed-loop system, significantly improving both the sustainability and economics of PET recycling. The advancements enable complete depolymerization in about 50 hours and improve the recovery of ethylene glycol, a core PET component.

Innovation isn't limited to PET. In January 2025, researchers from King's College London developed a novel method for recycling poly(lactic acid) (PLA), a common bioplastic. Their protocol uses ionic liquids to dissolve the plastic, followed by degradation with a commercially available lipase enzyme (CaLB), opening doors for more diverse enzymatic recycling applications.

Even more recently, in September 2026, a team at the University of Konstanz identified a new "Pac-Man enzyme." This enzyme demonstrates the ability to degrade certain polyesters and bioplastics, and surprisingly, also exhibits antibiotic resistance properties, hinting at complex microbial adaptations to synthetic materials. Adding another layer to sustainable solutions, August 2024 saw the introduction of "living plastics" by a group at the Shenzhen Institute of Advanced Technology. These materials incorporate engineered *Bacillus subtilis* spores that produce plastic-degrading enzymes when triggered by erosion or composting, leading to near-complete breakdown.

These "live" advancements represent a paradigm shift, transforming plastic waste from an environmental burden into a valuable resource. The continuous engineering of highly efficient enzymes, coupled with optimized processes, is paving the way for a truly circular economy for plastics.
